Dandelion

Taraxacum officinale

Bitter • Earthy • Roasted

The weed in every British lawn, and a traditional bitter tea. What dandelion root and leaf tea are, how to make them, what research says and who should take care.

Quick answer

What is dandelion tea good for?

Dandelion tea is made from the leaves or roots of Taraxacum officinale. Dandelion root tea is bitter and earthy, and roasted root is drunk as a caffeine-free “coffee”. European regulators accept dandelion’s traditional use for mild digestive complaints such as fullness and wind, and to increase urine flow. Human clinical trials are scarce, so other health claims are unproven.

Side effects & who should take care

  • Avoid if you are allergic to daisy-family plants (ragweed, chamomile, marigold).
  • Not recommended with gallstones, blocked bile ducts or liver disease.
  • It can increase urine; take care with diuretics or lithium and ask a pharmacist.
  • Only forage dandelions away from roads and areas sprayed with weedkiller.

Dandelion At A Glance

What is dandelion?

Dandelion is a hardy perennial in the daisy family, Asteraceae. Every part is edible, and the leaves and roots are used for tea.

Botanical Name Taraxacum officinale
Family Asteraceae (daisy family)
Parts Used Root, leaves & flowers
Character Bitter, earthy
Caffeine None, even when roasted

Dandelion tea, dandelion root tea and the evidence

Most people know dandelion as a lawn weed. Herbal traditions across Europe treat it as a valuable bitter plant.

The top questions are how to make dandelion tea, how to make dandelion root tea and what it is good for.

European regulators accept its traditional use for mild digestive complaints and to increase urine flow. That is recognition of long use, not proof from large trials.

01 • Know The Plant

From lawn weed to bitter tonic.

Dandelion grows a rosette of deeply toothed leaves from a long, thick taproot. The name comes from the French dent de lion, lion’s tooth.

The leaves are bitter and green, and are eaten in salads in spring. The root is darker and earthier, and is dried or roasted for tea.

Roasted dandelion root makes a dark, coffee-like drink with no caffeine, often sold as dandelion coffee.

Dandelion belongs to the daisy family, which matters for people with plant allergies.

Lion’s tooth in the leaf. Coffee colour in the root. No caffeine in either.

02 • Dandelion Tea Benefits

What does dandelion tea do?

Dandelion is a classic bitter herb. Its traditional uses centre on digestion and on increasing urine.

The honest picture: accepted traditional uses, very little clinical research.

European regulators accept dandelion for mild digestive complaints and to increase urine flow, based on long-standing use. Popular online claims about detox, liver cleansing, weight loss and cancer are not supported by human trials.

Accepted Traditional Use

Mild digestive complaints

Dandelion root is accepted for the relief of mild digestive complaints such as fullness, wind and slow digestion, based on traditional use.

Accepted Traditional Use

Increasing urine flow

Dandelion is traditionally used to increase the amount of urine to help flush the urinary tract.

Everyday Benefit

A caffeine-free coffee swap

Roasted dandelion root makes a dark, bitter drink with no caffeine.

04 • British & European Heritage

A weed with a long history in the teapot.

Dandelion grows across Europe and Asia and has spread to almost every temperate part of the world.

European herbalists have used it for centuries as a bitter and a diuretic. Its old French nickname, pissenlit, refers to that effect.

In Britain it lives on in dandelion and burdock, one of the country’s oldest soft drinks, and in a revival of foraging and dandelion coffee.

Native Range

Europe and Asia; now worldwide.

Name

Classic Bitter

06 • Dandelion Research Areas

Where researchers have looked.

These are areas researchers have studied, mostly in the lab and in animals. They do not mean dandelion tea treats these conditions.

Research Area 01

Digestion and bile flow

Dandelion’s bitter compounds may stimulate digestive and bile secretions, the basis of its traditional use.

Research Area 02

Urine output

A small human study reported increased urine frequency after dandelion leaf extract.

Research Area 03

Blood sugar and inflammation

Lab and animal studies have looked at blood sugar and inflammation. Human evidence is lacking.

07 • What Modern Research Says

Long tradition. Little clinical proof.

Dandelion has a strong place in herbal tradition but very few good human trials.

Regulatory Review

Traditional use accepted

The EMA herbal committee accepts dandelion root for mild digestive complaints, and dandelion leaf to increase urine flow, based on traditional use.

Key Limitation

Few human trials

Most research uses extracts in the lab or animals. Claims about detox, cancer and weight loss are not supported in people.

Safety Note

Bile and allergy cautions

Regulators advise against dandelion root with bile duct obstruction, gallstones or liver disease, and in daisy-family allergy.

10 • How To Make Dandelion Root Tea

How to make dandelion root tea.

Roots need simmering, not just steeping. Leaves can be steeped like ordinary tea.

Dandelion root tea (and leaf tea)

For root tea: add 1 to 2 teaspoons of dried, chopped dandelion root to about 300 ml of water in a small pan.

Bring to the boil, then simmer gently for 10 to 15 minutes. Use roasted root for a coffee-like flavour.

Strain and drink. A little honey or milk softens the bitterness.

For leaf tea: steep 1 to 2 teaspoons of dried leaf in 250 ml of just-boiled water for 5 to 10 minutes.

11 • Dandelion Side Effects & Safety

A weed, but still a herb.

Allergy, bile and medicines.

Dandelion is in the daisy family. Avoid it if you are allergic to ragweed, chamomile, marigolds or other daisy-family plants.

European regulators do not recommend dandelion root for people with bile duct obstruction, gallstones, liver disease or other bile problems.

Because dandelion increases urine, take care if you use diuretics, lithium or medicines for blood pressure, and ask a pharmacist.

If you forage, pick only from clean places away from roads, dogs and weedkiller.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, speak to a pharmacist or doctor before using dandelion medicinally. EU herbal medicines containing dandelion are generally for adults and children over 12.

The Rootz Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace medical advice.

12 • Questions People Ask About Dandelion

Dandelion tea questions, answered clearly.

Straightforward answers to the questions people search for most.

How do you make dandelion root tea?
Simmer 1 to 2 teaspoons of dried, chopped dandelion root in about 300 ml of water for 10 to 15 minutes, then strain. Use roasted root for a coffee-like drink.
How do you make dandelion tea from leaves?
Steep 1 to 2 teaspoons of dried leaves, or a small handful of fresh, clean leaves, in 250 ml of just-boiled water for 5 to 10 minutes, then strain.
What is dandelion tea good for?
It is traditionally used for mild digestive complaints such as fullness and wind, and to increase urine flow. European regulators accept these traditional uses.
Is dandelion tea good for you?
For most people it is a safe, caffeine-free bitter tea. It is not suitable for people with daisy-family allergies or bile problems.
What does dandelion root tea do?
Dandelion root is traditionally used to support digestion. Its bitter compounds may stimulate digestive and bile secretions.
Does dandelion tea detox your liver?
There is no good human evidence that dandelion tea detoxes the liver. Your liver and kidneys do that job themselves.
Is dandelion tea a diuretic?
Yes. Dandelion, especially the leaf, is traditionally used to increase urine, and one small human study supports this.
Does dandelion root tea have caffeine?
No. Even roasted dandelion “coffee” is naturally caffeine-free.
Can I pick dandelions from my garden for tea?
Yes, if your garden is not treated with weedkiller and is away from roads and pets. Make sure you have identified dandelion correctly.
Where can I buy dandelion root tea?
Dandelion root tea and roasted dandelion coffee are sold in UK health food shops, some supermarkets and online.
