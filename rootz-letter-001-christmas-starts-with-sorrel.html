What the research really says

Hibiscus and blood pressure, honestly.

The honest version: hibiscus is one of the few herbs with real human trials behind it. Reviews suggest it may lower blood pressure a little, mostly in people whose readings are already raised. That’s the only kind of claim you’ll get here.

If you take blood pressure tablets, tell your GP or pharmacist you drink hibiscus, and never swap your medicine for tea. Pregnant? Ask your midwife first: there isn’t enough evidence on hibiscus in pregnancy.

Black African and Caribbean people have a higher risk of high blood pressure, often with no symptoms, so get yours checked: your GP can do it at any age, and many pharmacies do it free if you’re 40 or over.

Herb of the week

Hibiscus (sorrel)

The deep red “flowers” you steep for sorrel are not petals. They are the calyces of Hibiscus sabdariffa, the fleshy cup that holds the seed pod.

  • One plant, many names. Sorrel in Jamaica and across the Caribbean, zobo in Nigeria, bissap in Senegal, sobolo in Ghana, karkadé in Egypt and Sudan.
  • It travelled with us. Roselle drinks were already part of life in West Africa, and the plant became one of the Caribbean’s best-loved festive drinks. Caribbean families then carried it to Britain, Canada and the US.
  • No caffeine. Tart, fruity and naturally caffeine-free.

Move • exercise

The 10-minute simmer stretch

Your sorrel needs exactly 10 minutes on the boil. So do you. Set a timer and stay near the pot:

  1. 2 minutes: march on the spot, swinging your arms.
  2. 2 minutes: sit-to-stands from a kitchen chair, slow and steady.
  3. 2 minutes: press-ups against the kitchen counter.
  4. 2 minutes: calf raises, holding the counter for balance.
  5. 2 minutes: gentle stretches: reach up tall, then roll your shoulders and neck.

Timer goes, sorrel’s done, and you’ve moved for 10 minutes. Then pour a cup of Rise.

New to exercise or managing a health condition? Start gently, and stop if anything hurts.

Still • meditation

A practice batch and four quiet minutes

Christmas is a few weeks away, so make a practice batch now: drink it this week and get your spices just how you like them. Homemade sorrel is best drunk within a few days, so we’ll send the timing for your Christmas batch on 13 December.

  1. Sunday afternoon: rinse the dried sorrel and put it in your biggest pot with the ginger and spices.
  2. Bring it to the boil, then simmer for 10 minutes. Turn off the heat and cover.
  3. While it steeps, take 4 minutes for yourself. Sit somewhere you can still smell the pot. Breathe in for a count of 4, hold for 4, out for 6. Repeat until the kitchen timer goes, or until you remember a Christmas from when you were small.
  4. Leave it overnight. Strain on Monday, sweeten to taste, bottle and chill.

Pour a cup of Cool Mind while you wait.

The kitchen

Jamaican sorrel, or the zobo way

Makes about 2 litres (8 glasses).

  • 60 g dried sorrel (hibiscus calyces)
  • 60 g fresh ginger, grated or thinly sliced
  • 10 to 12 pimento (allspice) berries
  • 6 cloves and 1 cinnamon stick
  • Peel of 1 orange (optional)
  • 2 litres water
  • About 150 g sugar, to taste, and the juice of 1 lime (optional)

Simmer everything except the sugar and lime for 10 minutes, cover and steep for at least 8 hours or overnight. Strain, sweeten, add lime, chill and serve over ice. Rum is traditional in many homes; the drink is just as good without.

Tip: sweeten after steeping, not before. You’ll use less sugar.

The zobo way (Nigeria): use the same dried leaves, leave out the pimento, and simmer with chunks of pineapple (skin and all, well washed), extra ginger and a few cloves. Strain, sweeten lightly and chill. No rum: zobo is an all-year, all-ages drink.

Where to buy in the UK: dried sorrel, also sold as zobo leaves or hibiscus, is at most African and Caribbean grocers, in markets like Brixton and Ridley Road, and in the world foods aisle of bigger supermarkets.

Share the pot: this makes 8 glasses, so it was always meant to be shared. Bottle some for a neighbour, the office party or Christmas dinner. New to it? Buy your leaves from a Black-owned grocer and ask how they make theirs.

Culture

Christmas in a glass, from the islands to the motherland

Ask any Caribbean or African family whose version is best and you’ll start an argument. Here’s how the islands do it:

  • Jamaica: sorrel with plenty of ginger and pimento, often with a splash of rum.
  • Barbados: sorrel with a sharper ginger kick.
  • Grenada: nutmeg, the spice island’s signature, finds its way in.
  • Trinidad and Tobago: sorrel sits beside ponche de crème, a rich chilled cream punch of milk, eggs, sugar, rum and Angostura bitters.
  • Haiti: kremas, a creamy, spiced coconut drink.
  • Puerto Rico and the Dominican Republic: coquito, the coconut cousin of eggnog.
  • Guyana: sorrel and homemade ginger beer on the table, with pepperpot for Christmas morning.

And in West Africa it’s zobo in Nigeria, sobolo in Ghana and bissap in Senegal, drunk all year round. December is also when many British Nigerians and Ghanaians fly home: “Detty December” (“detty” is Nigerian pidgin for “dirty”, as in a full-on party season) in Lagos and Accra has become one of the biggest diaspora seasons of the year.

Travel and history

Jamaica’s Grand Market

On Christmas Eve, the main towns across Jamaica turn into one big street fair. Gran’ Market (Jamaican for Grand Market) starts in the day and runs all night into Christmas morning: stalls, street food, music, new clothes and the whole community out together.

Its roots go back to slavery. Christmas was one of the few times enslaved people were allowed to sell the produce they had grown themselves, and the market grew from that small slice of freedom. Around the same season you’d see Jonkonnu: masked, costumed dancers whose tradition mixes West African masquerade with British folk custom.

If you go: Santa Cruz in St Elizabeth claims the biggest Grand Market in Jamaica.

This side of the water: there’s no Gran’ Market in Manchester, but the Manchester Christmas Markets fill the city centre until 22 December, with Albert Square and Cathedral Gardens open until 3 January.

Her Rootz

Fibroids, and why they’re worth talking about

Fibroids are non-cancerous growths in or around the womb. They’re very common: around 2 in 3 women will have at least one at some point, most often between 30 and 50. The NHS notes they’re more common in women of African-Caribbean origin. In a recent survey of women in England, about 1 in 5 Black women said they’d had fibroids, compared with 1 in 20 white women.

Many women have no symptoms. When they do, look out for:

  • heavy or painful periods
  • pain in your tummy or lower back
  • needing to wee often, or constipation
  • pain or discomfort during sex

What to do: if your periods are heavy enough to affect daily life, see your GP. Keep a simple period diary for a couple of months (how many days, how heavy, how much pain) and take it with you. It makes the conversation faster and harder to brush off.

His Rootz

One in four (it’s Movember)

November is Movember, and Thursday 19 November was International Men’s Day. So here’s the number every Black man in the UK should know: 1 in 4 Black men will get prostate cancer in their lifetime, double the 1 in 8 for all men, according to Prostate Cancer UK. Whoever you are, 1 in 8 is reason enough to know your family history.

Early prostate cancer often has no symptoms, which is why knowing your risk matters more than waiting for signs. Recent national audit data also shows Black men in England are more likely to be diagnosed at a later stage.

  • Under 45: know the 1 in 4 now. If your dad, brother or uncle has had prostate cancer, tell your GP, who can talk to you about testing. 45 or over: ask your GP about your risk and the PSA blood test. You’re allowed to ask.
  • Take Prostate Cancer UK’s 30-second online risk checker.
  • Forward this to your dad, uncles and brothers. That’s the real Movember.

Future Rootz • launch edition

Hurricane Melissa and the pimento in your sorrel

Future Rootz looks ahead at the big changes coming and what they mean for our people, our plants and our communities. It appears on the first Sunday of each month.

If your family was affected by Hurricane Melissa, we’re thinking of you. The pimento berries in this week’s sorrel most likely came from Jamaica. On 28 October 2025, Category 5 Hurricane Melissa hit the island, the strongest climate disaster in its modern history.

  • Farm losses were estimated at J$30 billion, with around 70,000 farmers affected, hardest in the western parishes.
  • About 70% of pimento crops in key growing areas were destroyed, and the commercial banana and plantain crop was wiped out.
  • Quick crops like vegetables bounced back within months. Tree crops like pimento take years to replant and grow.

What it means for us: stronger storms are hitting the places our herbs and spices come from. That affects farmers’ livelihoods first, and in time what reaches our kitchens.

One practical takeaway: when you buy pimento or ginger this Christmas, look for Jamaican-grown or Caribbean-owned brands. Buying their produce is direct support while farmers replant.

Give directly: a year on, the Red Cross recovery appeal for Jamaica is still only 62% funded. It now gives farmers, fishers and small traders grants to replant, repair and restart.
