Bitter Kola

Garcinia kola

Bitter • Astringent • Woody

A seed of welcome and ceremony across West and Central Africa. What bitter kola is, how it is used, what research really says and who should take care.

Quick answer

What is bitter kola used for?

Bitter kola is the seed of Garcinia kola, a forest tree native to West and Central Africa. It is traditionally chewed raw, offered to guests and used in folk remedies for coughs, sore throats and stomach upsets. Laboratory and animal studies have explored its compounds, especially kolaviron, but there are almost no human trials, so its health claims are unproven.

Side effects & who should take care

  • There is no established safe daily amount; most people chew one or two seeds occasionally.
  • Avoid medicinal use if you are pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding; human safety data are lacking.
  • High-dose animal studies have raised questions about fertility, liver and kidney effects.
  • Check with a pharmacist if you take regular medicines, including for blood pressure or diabetes.

Bitter Kola At A Glance

What is bitter kola?

Bitter kola is the seed of Garcinia kola, a tree in the Clusiaceae family that grows in the moist lowland forests of West and Central Africa.

Botanical Name Garcinia kola
Family Clusiaceae
Part Used Seeds (nuts)
Character Bitter & astringent
Native Range West & Central Africa

Bitter kola, kola nut and West African tradition

Bitter kola is one of West Africa’s most recognisable seeds. It is chewed raw, shared with guests and carried in pockets and bags.

Despite the name, it is not the same plant as kola nut, which comes from Cola trees and is best known as a source of caffeine.

Bitter kola is often called a “wonder plant”. The research is interesting, but almost all of it comes from laboratories and animals, not people.

01 • Know The Seed

A bitter seed with a big reputation.

Garcinia kola is a medium-sized evergreen forest tree. Its orange fruit holds several brown, kidney-shaped seeds, and these seeds are bitter kola.

Bite into one and the bitterness arrives first, followed by a drying, astringent feel. Many people say the taste mellows into a faint sweetness afterwards.

In Nigeria it is orogbo in Yoruba, aki ilu in Igbo and namijin goro, “male kola”, in Hausa.

It is sometimes called false kola because it is not a true kola. Kola nut comes from a different genus, Cola.

Not a kola nut. A different tree, a different seed and its own tradition.

02 • Bitter Kola Benefits

What is bitter kola good for?

Bitter kola has a wide traditional reputation. Modern research has focused on a group of compounds called kolaviron, studied mostly in the lab.

The biggest gap: almost no human trials.

Scientific reviews of Garcinia kola note the absence of proper human clinical trials. Many lab studies also used doses far higher than anyone would get from chewing a seed. Promising results in cells or rats are not proof of benefit in people.

Traditionally Associated

Throat and chest

Bitter kola is traditionally chewed for coughs, hoarseness and sore throats across West Africa. This is cultural use, not a proven treatment.

Laboratory Research

Kolaviron

Kolaviron, a group of biflavonoids found in the seeds, has shown antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and liver-protective activity in lab and animal studies.

Evidence Context

Seed versus extract

Research usually uses purified extracts at high doses. A chewed seed or a cup of bitter kola tea is a very different exposure.

04 • West African Heritage

A seed of welcome, carried across the diaspora.

Bitter kola grows in the forests of Nigeria, Ghana, Cameroon and neighbouring countries, where it has been used for generations.

Its place is social as much as medicinal. Offering bitter kola is a gesture of welcome, and it appears in ceremonies, prayers and everyday hospitality.

Through migration it travelled with West African communities to Britain. Today it is sold in African grocers across the UK and is one of the most searched-for African botanicals.

Native Range

Many Names

06 • Garcinia Kola Research Areas

Where researchers have looked.

These are areas researchers have studied, mostly in laboratories and animals. They do not mean bitter kola treats these conditions.

Research Area 01

Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity

Kolaviron and related compounds reduce markers of oxidative stress and inflammation in lab and animal studies.

Research Area 02

Liver protection

Animal studies suggest kolaviron may protect the liver from some toxins. Human evidence is lacking.

Research Area 03

Antimicrobial activity

Seed extracts slow the growth of some bacteria in the lab, which may explain traditional use for throats and coughs.

07 • What Modern Research Says

Promising chemistry. Weak human evidence.

Bitter kola has a large laboratory literature but very little clinical research. Reviewers are clear that well-designed human trials are still needed.

Critical Review

No human clinical trials

A 2023 critical review of Garcinia kola chemistry and pharmacology noted the absence of human clinical trials and flaws such as very high test doses.

Safety Gap

Dose not established

Reviews state that the standard dose and safety of the seed still need to be established in people.

Key Limitation

Seed ≠ purified extract

Even positive lab results use purified compounds or concentrated extracts, not traditional chewing or tea.

08 • Three Ways To Read The Evidence

Tradition. Science. The unknown.

Rooted In Tradition

A seed of welcome.

Bitter kola has deep cultural meaning across West Africa. That heritage deserves to be recorded properly.

Studied By Science

Its compounds are active.

Kolaviron and other compounds show real biological activity in laboratory and animal research.

Not Yet Proven

Bitter kola is not a cure.

There is no human evidence that bitter kola cures infections, lowers blood sugar or treats any disease.

10 • How To Prepare Bitter Kola

How to prepare bitter kola.

Bitter kola is mainly chewed. If you prefer a drink, a simple infusion is the gentlest option. This is a culinary-style method, not a medicinal dose.

Chewed or as a simple infusion

To chew: peel off the thin brown skin of one seed and chew slowly. Expect strong bitterness that softens over time.

For tea: thinly slice or grate half to one seed into a cup.

Pour over about 250 ml of freshly boiled water, cover and steep for 5 to 10 minutes, then strain.

Keep it occasional. There is no established safe daily amount.

11 • Bitter Kola Side Effects & Safety

Traditional does not mean tested.

Human safety data are limited.

Bitter kola has been chewed for generations, and occasional use is common. But it has not been properly studied for safety in people.

There is no established safe daily amount. Online claims about “two a day” or more come from tradition, not research.

Some high-dose animal studies have raised questions about effects on male fertility, the liver and the kidneys. These results may not apply to normal use, but they are a reason not to take large amounts or concentrated extracts.

Because its effects on blood pressure, blood sugar and medicines have not been tested properly in people, take care if you use regular medication.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, avoid medicinal use of bitter kola and speak to a doctor, midwife or pharmacist first. Do not give bitter kola to young children.

The Rootz Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace medical advice.

12 • Questions People Ask About Bitter Kola

Bitter kola questions, answered clearly.

Straightforward answers to the questions people search for most.

What is bitter kola?
Bitter kola is the seed of Garcinia kola, a forest tree native to West and Central Africa. It is chewed raw for its bitter taste and has a long place in hospitality and folk medicine.
What is bitter kola used for?
Traditionally it is chewed for coughs, sore throats and stomach upsets, offered to guests, and used as a general tonic. These are cultural uses; none is proven by human trials.
What are the health benefits of bitter kola?
Lab and animal studies suggest its compounds, especially kolaviron, have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, liver-protective and antimicrobial activity. These benefits have not been confirmed in people.
Is bitter kola the same as kola nut?
No. Bitter kola comes from Garcinia kola. Kola nut comes from Cola trees and is best known as a source of caffeine. They are different plants used in similar social settings.
How many bitter kola can I eat a day?
There is no established safe daily amount. Most people chew one or two seeds occasionally. Avoid eating large amounts regularly.
Does bitter kola lower blood sugar?
Some animal studies suggest an effect, but this has not been shown in proper human trials. Do not use bitter kola in place of diabetes treatment.
Can bitter kola raise or lower blood pressure?
There is not enough human research to say. If you have high or low blood pressure or take blood pressure medicine, ask your doctor or pharmacist before using it regularly.
Can a pregnant woman eat bitter kola?
There is not enough safety information for pregnancy. Avoid medicinal use and speak to your midwife or doctor before eating it during pregnancy or while breastfeeding.
Can bitter kola cure a cough or sore throat?
It is traditionally chewed for coughs and sore throats, and extracts show antibacterial activity in the lab, but it has not been proven to cure infections. See a doctor if symptoms are severe or last more than three weeks.
Where can I buy bitter kola in the UK?
Bitter kola is sold in African grocers and markets across the UK and by online African food suppliers.
