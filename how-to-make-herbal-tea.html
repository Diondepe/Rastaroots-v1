How To Make Herbal Tea

Ratios • Steep Times • Blends

Everything you need to brew a great cup from fresh or dried herbs: how much to use, how long to steep, when to simmer, how to blend, and which herb to choose.

Start Brewing ↓ Herbal Library

Quick answer

How do you make herbal tea?

Use about 1 teaspoon of dried herb or 1 tablespoon of fresh herb per 250 ml cup. Pour over freshly boiled water, cover, and steep for 5 to 10 minutes. Roots, bark and hard seeds, such as ginger or dandelion root, should be simmered for 10 to 20 minutes instead. Strain, taste and adjust.

The method in five steps

  1. Choose a correctly identified, food-grade herb.
  2. Measure 1 tsp dried or 1 tbsp fresh per cup.
  3. Pour over freshly boiled water.
  4. Cover and steep 5–10 minutes (simmer roots 10–20).
  5. Strain, taste and adjust.

01 • The Golden Ratios

How much herb per cup?

Start here and adjust to taste. These are culinary amounts for an everyday cup, not medicinal doses.

01 • Dried

1 teaspoon per cup

About 1 to 2 g of dried leaf or flower per 250 ml. Dried herbs are concentrated.

02 • Fresh

1 tablespoon per cup

About three times the dried amount: a small handful of fresh leaves, lightly crushed.

03 • Roots & Bark

1–2 teaspoons, simmered

Hard materials such as ginger, dandelion root and sarsaparilla need simmering, not just steeping.

04 • Tea Bags

1 bag per cup

Follow the pack, but covering the cup and steeping a little longer usually helps.

02 • Infusion Or Decoction?

Soft parts steep. Hard parts simmer.

The part of the plant decides the method. Getting this right is the biggest difference between a weak cup and a great one.

Infusion

Leaves & flowers: steep

Pour freshly boiled water over the herb, cover and leave 5–10 minutes. For mint, lemon balm, nettle, hibiscus, rooibos and most herbal teas.

Decoction

Roots, bark & seeds: simmer

Add the herb to cold water, bring to the boil and simmer gently for 10–20 minutes. For ginger, dandelion root, sarsaparilla and mauby bark.

Cold Brew

Delicate or tart herbs: steep cold

Steep in cold water in the fridge for 6–12 hours for a smooth, less bitter drink. Works well for hibiscus and mint.

03 • Step By Step

How to make herbal tea, the simple way.

A basic herbal infusion

Choose a herb you have correctly identified and that is sold for food or tea use.

Measure about 1 teaspoon dried or 1 tablespoon fresh per 250 ml cup.

Pour over freshly boiled water. For delicate green leaves, let it cool for a minute first.

Cover the cup or pot to keep the aromatic oils in, and steep for 5 to 10 minutes.

Strain, taste and adjust. Add honey, lemon or a slice of ginger if you like.

04 • Fresh Or Dried?

Garden-fresh or from the jar.

Fresh

Brighter, softer flavour

Best for mint, lemon balm, nettle tops and lemongrass. Use about three times the dried amount and lightly bruise the leaves first.

Dried

Stronger, available all year

More concentrated and easier to store. Keep in an airtight jar, away from light and heat, and use within about a year.

Drying Your Own

Simple air-drying

Hang small bunches upside down in a warm, airy, shaded place for 1–2 weeks until crisp, then crumble into jars.

06 • How To Blend

Build your own blend: base, support, accent.

A simple way to blend is two parts base, one part support and half a part accent. Taste as you go.

Caffeine-Free Cape

Rooibos + honeybush + orange peel

Two parts rooibos, one part honeybush, a little dried orange peel. Sweet, smooth, any time of day.

Warming

Ginger + lemongrass + cinnamon

Two parts sliced ginger, one part lemongrass, a small piece of cinnamon. Simmer briefly for a warming cup.

Caribbean Ruby

Hibiscus + ginger + clove

Two parts hibiscus, one part ginger, a clove or two. A sorrel-style tea, lovely iced.

Garden Calm

Lemon balm + spearmint

Two parts lemon balm, one part spearmint. Fresh, lemony and gentle.

After Dinner

Peppermint + ginger

Two parts peppermint, half a part ginger. Cooling with a warm finish.

Spring Green

Nettle + mint + lemon

Two parts nettle, one part mint, a slice of lemon. A bright foraged blend.

07 • Brew Safely

Natural does not always mean harmless.

Five simple rules.

Identify plants correctly. Only forage plants you can identify with certainty, from clean places away from roads and weedkiller.

Buy food-grade herbs. Use herbs sold for food or tea, not for crafts, potpourri or gardens.

Check pregnancy advice. The NHS advises no more than 4 cups of herbal tea a day in pregnancy, and avoiding teas that contain liquorice root. Some herbs should be avoided altogether; ask your midwife.

Check your medicines. Some herbs interact with medicines for blood pressure, diabetes, blood thinning or mood. Ask a pharmacist.

Tea is not treatment. Herbal tea can be part of a healthy routine, but it does not replace medical care. See a doctor about symptoms that persist.

08 • Questions People Ask

Herbal tea questions, answered clearly.

How do you make herbal tea?
Use about 1 teaspoon of dried herb or 1 tablespoon of fresh herb per 250 ml cup. Pour over freshly boiled water, cover, and steep for 5 to 10 minutes. Roots, bark and seeds should be simmered for 10 to 20 minutes instead. Strain and drink.
What is herbal tea?
Herbal tea, also called a tisane or infusion, is any drink made by steeping plants other than the tea plant, Camellia sinensis, in hot water. It can use leaves, flowers, roots, seeds or bark.
Does herbal tea have caffeine?
Most herbal teas are naturally caffeine-free. Exceptions are blends that contain black or green tea, and a few caffeinated plants such as yerba mate and guayusa. Check the ingredients.
Is green tea a herbal tea?
No. Green, black, white and oolong teas all come from the tea plant, Camellia sinensis, and contain caffeine. Herbal teas come from other plants.
Is herbal tea good for you?
Plain herbal tea is a calorie-free, usually caffeine-free way to drink more fluid. Some herbs have accepted traditional uses, but herbal tea is not a medicine, and some herbs are not suitable during pregnancy or with certain medicines.
Does herbal tea count towards water intake?
Yes. NHS guidance says all non-alcoholic drinks, including tea, count towards your daily fluid intake.
Does herbal tea break a fast?
Plain herbal tea with no sugar, honey or milk contains almost no calories, so many people drink it during fasting. Adding sweeteners or milk changes that.
How long should you steep herbal tea?
Leaves and flowers: 5 to 10 minutes, covered. Roots, bark and hard seeds: simmer for 10 to 20 minutes. Low-tannin teas such as rooibos and honeybush can steep longer without turning bitter.
Should I use fresh or dried herbs?
Both work. Dried herbs are more concentrated, so use about 1 teaspoon per cup. Use roughly three times as much fresh herb, about 1 tablespoon or a small handful.
Why should I cover herbal tea while it steeps?
Many herbs, such as mint, lemon balm and lemongrass, get their flavour from aromatic oils that escape with the steam. Covering the cup keeps them in.
How many cups of herbal tea a day is safe?
Most healthy adults can enjoy several cups of common herbal teas a day. In pregnancy, the NHS advises no more than 4 cups of herbal tea a day and avoiding teas containing liquorice root. Some herbs have specific cautions, so check each herb’s safety notes.
Can I reuse herbal tea leaves?
Yes, many herbs, especially rooibos, honeybush and roots, can be steeped a second time, though the second cup is milder.
