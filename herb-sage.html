Sage

Salvia officinalis

Earthy • Herbal • Slightly Bitter

The soft grey-green herb of stuffing and roast dinners, and a traditional tea for menopause hot flushes. How to make sage tea, what the research shows, and why the dose matters.

Quick answer

Is sage tea good for you?

Sage tea is made from the leaves of Salvia officinalis (common sage). It is earthy, herbal and caffeine-free. A few small studies suggest sage might reduce how often menopause hot flushes happen, but the evidence is limited. Sage contains thujone, which can be toxic in large amounts, so stick to a cup or two a day and avoid concentrated sage oil.

Side effects & who should take care

  • One or two cups a day is a sensible amount for most adults.
  • Avoid large amounts and sage oil: thujone can be toxic.
  • Avoid medicinal amounts if you are pregnant, breastfeeding or have epilepsy.
  • Ask a pharmacist if you take diabetes or seizure medicines.

Sage At A Glance

What is sage?

Sage is a woody, evergreen herb in the mint family with soft, grey-green leaves. The word “sage” covers many Salvia plants; common sage is the one used in cooking and tea.

Botanical Name Salvia officinalis (common sage)
Family Lamiaceae (mint family)
Part Used Leaves
Character Earthy, herbal, warm
Native Range Mediterranean region

Sage, menopause and the evidence

Sage has been used as a food and remedy since ancient Greece and Rome.

Its Latin name comes from salvere, “to be well”.

Small studies suggest it may help hot flushes. It is not a replacement for menopause care from your GP.

01 • Know The Leaf

A velvet leaf with a long history.

Common sage is a hardy shrub with soft, velvety, grey-green leaves and purple-blue flowers.

It belongs to the mint family and the large Salvia genus, which also now includes rosemary.

Sage leaves are used in stuffing, roast dinners, butter sauces and tea.

In the Caribbean, “sage” or “wild sage” can mean different bush plants, some of which are not safe to drink. Check what you are buying.

Many plants are called sage. Know which one is in your cup.

02 • Sage Benefits

What is sage tea good for?

Sage is best known today as a traditional tea for menopause hot flushes.

The honest picture: promising small studies, not a treatment.

A few small studies suggest common sage might reduce how often hot flushes happen during menopause, and a 2023 review linked sage to fewer hot flushes but not milder ones. Most studies used sage preparations or extracts rather than tea. Some studies have also looked at blood fats. If menopause symptoms affect your life, talk to your GP about treatments that work.

04 • World Heritage

From the Mediterranean to the Sunday roast.

Common sage grows wild on dry, sunny slopes around the Mediterranean, particularly along the Adriatic coast.

It was valued in ancient Greece and Rome, and its Latin name, Salvia, comes from salvere, “to be well”.

Sage has grown in British gardens for centuries, and sage and onion stuffing is a fixture of the Christmas dinner table.

10 • How To Make Sage Tea

How to make sage tea.

Sage is strong and slightly bitter. A few leaves are enough.

Sage tea

Use 4 to 5 fresh sage leaves, lightly bruised, or 1 teaspoon of dried sage.

Put them in a cup and pour over 250 ml of just-boiled water.

Cover and steep for 5 minutes, then strain.

Add lemon and a little honey to soften the bitterness. Keep to one or two cups a day.

Step 01 • Measure 4–5 fresh leaves or 1 tsp dried.
Step 02 • Pour 250 ml just-boiled water.
Step 03 • Steep 5 minutes, covered.
Step 04 • Soften Lemon and honey to taste.

11 • Sage Side Effects & Safety

A kitchen herb with a limit.

Thujone, pregnancy, breastfeeding and epilepsy.

Sage in food and a cup or two of tea is likely safe for most adults.

Thujone: common sage contains thujone, which can be toxic in large amounts and has been linked to seizures. Avoid sage oil and do not drink large amounts every day for long periods.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding: avoid medicinal amounts. Sage is traditionally used to reduce breast milk, so it is best avoided while breastfeeding.

Epilepsy and medicines: avoid medicinal amounts if you have epilepsy. Sage may affect blood sugar and could interact with diabetes and seizure medicines.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, speak to a pharmacist or doctor before using sage supplements, and talk to your GP about menopause symptoms rather than relying on sage alone.

The Rootz Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace medical advice.

12 • Questions People Ask About Sage

Sage tea questions, answered clearly.

Straightforward answers to the questions people search for most.

Is sage tea good for you?
Sage tea is a caffeine-free herbal drink. Small studies suggest sage may reduce menopause hot flushes, but evidence is limited.
Does sage tea help menopause?
A few small studies suggest sage might reduce how often hot flushes happen. It is not a replacement for menopause care from your GP.
How do you make sage tea?
Steep 4 to 5 fresh leaves or 1 teaspoon of dried sage in 250 ml of just-boiled water for 5 minutes, covered, then strain.
How much sage tea is safe a day?
One or two cups a day is sensible for most adults. Avoid large daily amounts and sage oil because of thujone.
Is sage tea safe while breastfeeding?
It is best avoided. Sage is traditionally used to reduce breast milk supply.
Is sage safe in pregnancy?
Sage in food is fine. Avoid medicinal amounts and sage oil while pregnant.
What is thujone?
Thujone is a natural compound in common sage that can be toxic in large amounts and has been linked to seizures.
Is sage good for a sore throat?
Sage is used as a traditional gargle. Evidence is limited; see a GP if a sore throat is severe or lasts more than a week.
Is “wild sage” in the Caribbean the same plant?
Not always. Different bush plants are called sage or wild sage, and some are not safe to drink. Check the plant before using it.
Does sage tea contain caffeine?
No. Sage tea is naturally caffeine-free.
