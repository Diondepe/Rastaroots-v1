What is the RastaRoots Herbal Library?

The RastaRoots Herbal Library is an educational collection exploring plants used in herbal tea, traditional drinks and Caribbean botanical culture.

The founding collection contains ginger, lemongrass, mint, cerasee, bitter melon, moringa, hibiscus / sorrel and sarsaparilla.

Each botanical is explored through identity, flavour, preparation, heritage, traditional context, modern evidence, unanswered questions and safety. Traditional knowledge is respected without automatically turning traditional use into medical fact.

Why The Library Exists

Herbal knowledge deserves more than a product label.

RastaRoots is building beyond tea. The Herbal Library exists to reconnect ingredients with the places, communities, traditions and knowledge systems that helped shape how they are used.

Rooted In Tradition

Not Yet Proven

Rootz Library Questions

Start here.

What is the RastaRoots Herbal Library?
The RastaRoots Herbal Library is an educational botanical resource exploring herbal tea, plant identity, flavour, preparation, Caribbean and African heritage, traditional knowledge and modern research.
Which herbs are in the Library?
The founding eight are ginger, lemongrass, mint, cerasee, bitter melon, moringa, hibiscus / sorrel and sarsaparilla. All eight full entries are now live.
Is Rootz Library membership free?
Yes. Founding Rootz Library membership is free. Members can access Field Guide 001, botanical knowledge checks, Rootz Points and future Library releases.
What is herbal tea?
Herbal tea is a broad everyday term for drinks made by infusing or preparing botanicals such as leaves, flowers, roots, fruits, seeds or grasses in water. Preparation differs according to the plant material.
Does the Herbal Library give medical advice?
No. The Library is educational. It distinguishes traditional knowledge from modern scientific evidence and does not diagnose, treat or replace professional medical advice.
Why does RastaRoots separate traditional use from scientific evidence?
Traditional knowledge is historically and culturally valuable, but a traditional use does not automatically prove a modern clinical effect. Keeping those categories clear allows both heritage and evidence to be represented more accurately.
