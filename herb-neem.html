Neem

Azadirachta indica

Very Bitter • Green • Sharp

Dogonyaro in Nigeria, nimba in India. The bitter tree used across Africa, Asia and the Caribbean, what research really shows, and the serious safety warnings everyone should know.

Explore Neem ↓ Join The Library

Quick answer

Is neem safe to drink?

Neem (Azadirachta indica) is a fast-growing tree native to the Indian subcontinent and widely grown across Africa, where it is called dogonyaro in Nigeria. Its very bitter leaves are used in traditional medicine. Research is mostly in the lab and in animals. Neem oil and seeds are poisonous if swallowed, and have caused serious poisoning and deaths in young children. Neem is not a cure for malaria.

Side effects & who should take care

  • Never give neem oil, seeds or neem products by mouth to babies or children.
  • Avoid neem completely if pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive.
  • Large or long-term use may harm the liver and kidneys.
  • Malaria needs urgent medical treatment, not herbs.

Neem At A Glance

What is neem?

Neem is a large evergreen tree in the mahogany family. It grows quickly in hot, dry places and gives deep shade.

Botanical Name Azadirachta indica
Family Meliaceae (mahogany family)
Part Used Leaves (oil and bark used externally)
Character Very bitter
Native Range Indian subcontinent

Neem, Africa and the evidence

Neem is native to India and is now planted widely across Africa, including Nigeria, Ghana and East Africa.

In Nigeria it is called dogonyaro, and its leaves are boiled in traditional fever remedies.

Lab research is wide, but human evidence is limited, and neem oil is dangerous to swallow.

01 • Know The Tree

A shade tree with a bitter reputation.

Neem trees grow up to 20 metres tall, with narrow, toothed leaflets and small, sweet-smelling white flowers.

Its olive-like fruit holds seeds that are pressed for neem oil, used in soaps, shampoos and natural insecticides.

Neem twigs are used as chewing sticks for teeth in India and Africa.

The leaves are extremely bitter.

Great on the skin and in the soil. Treat it with respect in the cup.

02 • Neem Uses

What is neem used for?

Neem is used for skin, teeth, fever and pests in traditional medicine.

The honest picture: wide lab research, limited human proof, real dangers.

Neem has shown antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects in the lab, and neem creams may help repel mosquitoes on the skin. A neem shampoo has been studied for head lice. There is a lack of robust human trials showing neem treats malaria, and it should never replace malaria medicine. Swallowing neem oil or seeds can be very dangerous.

04 • African & Indian Heritage

From Indian villages to Nigerian compounds.

Neem has been used in India for thousands of years, where it is called nimba and is linked with health and protection.

It was planted across Africa for shade and firewood, and is now part of everyday life in Nigeria, where it is called dogonyaro or dongoyaro.

Today neem grows across the tropics, including parts of the Caribbean, and its oil is sold in UK shops in soaps and shampoos.

10 • How To Use Neem Safely

How to use neem leaves safely.

We do not recommend drinking neem regularly. If you do, keep it weak, short and adult-only.

Weak neem leaf infusion (adults only)

Use only a few fresh or dried neem leaves, never seeds or oil.

Steep 3 to 4 leaves in 250 ml of just-boiled water for 5 minutes, then strain.

Drink it only occasionally and for a few days at most.

Never give it to children, and avoid it if pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive.

11 • Neem Side Effects & Safety

Read this before using neem.

Children, pregnancy, liver and kidneys, malaria.

Children: taking neem seeds or oil by mouth is likely unsafe for children. Serious poisoning in infants, including vomiting, seizures, coma and death, has been reported within hours. Never give neem by mouth to a baby or child.

Pregnancy and fertility: neem oil and bark are likely unsafe in pregnancy, and neem may affect fertility. Avoid it if pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive.

Liver and kidneys: large doses or long-term use may harm the liver and kidneys.

Malaria: fever after travel to a malaria area is a medical emergency. Seek urgent help.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, speak to a doctor or pharmacist before taking neem by mouth, and call NHS 111 or 999 if a child swallows neem oil.

The Rootz Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace medical advice.
