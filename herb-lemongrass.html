Lemongrass At A Glance

What is lemongrass?

Lemongrass is Cymbopogon citratus, an aromatic perennial grass in the Poaceae family. Its long leaves and lower stalks release a distinctive citrus-like aroma.

Lemongrass tea, fever grass and modern research

Lemongrass originated in South Asia and later became established across many tropical regions, including Africa and the Caribbean.

In Caribbean communities, Cymbopogon citratus is commonly associated with the name fever grass and with household bush-tea traditions.

People commonly search for “lemongrass benefits,” “lemongrass tea benefits” and “what is lemongrass tea good for?” The Rootz Library separates traditional use, research and unsupported claims rather than treating them as the same thing.

01 • Know The Grass

What exactly is lemongrass?

Lemongrass is a clump-forming perennial grass whose long aromatic leaves can reach considerable height in warm tropical environments.

The characteristic lemon-like aroma is strongly associated with volatile compounds including citral. That fragrance explains much of lemongrass's importance in food, tea and aromatic traditions.

Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew recognises Cymbopogon citratus as an accepted species native to South India and Sri Lanka.

Citrus without citrus — a tropical grass remembered by its aroma.

02 • Lemongrass Benefits

What do people mean by “lemongrass benefits”?

Traditional use of lemongrass is extensive. Modern human evidence is considerably smaller. That distinction matters.

Rootz Library approach

Studies involving essential oil, concentrated extracts, animals or isolated compounds such as citral cannot automatically be used to make claims about an ordinary cup of lemongrass tea.

Human Research Exists

Cardiovascular outcomes

Small human studies and broader experimental research have investigated lemongrass tea, blood pressure and cardiovascular markers. This research remains much less established than the evidence base for conventional treatments.

Mostly Preclinical

Inflammatory & antimicrobial research

Lemongrass extracts and essential oils have produced interesting findings in laboratory and animal research. These results do not establish the same effects from drinking lemongrass tea.

Traditional Use

Relaxation & household tea

Lemongrass is often used as a pleasant, aromatic household infusion. Its traditional reputation as a relaxing drink should not be interpreted as proof that lemongrass tea treats anxiety or sleep disorders.

04 • Africa, Caribbean & Fever Grass

From tropical grass to household tea.

Lemongrass did not originate in Africa or the Caribbean. Cymbopogon citratus is native to South India and Sri Lanka.

Human cultivation carried the plant across warm tropical regions, where it became established in Africa, Central and South America and the Caribbean.

In Caribbean traditions, the plant became familiar as fever grass and as an everyday household bush tea. Its meaning therefore extends beyond botanical origin into cultural adoption.

South Asian Origins

The accepted native range includes South India and Sri Lanka.

Tropical Africa

Lemongrass became introduced across numerous African regions and incorporated into local food and herbal practices.

Caribbean

Warm Caribbean climates allowed lemongrass to become established across several islands.

Fever Grass

The name reflects an important Caribbean household tradition, while not itself proving a medical effect.

06 • Lemongrass Research Areas

Where researchers have looked.

These markers identify broad areas of research. They do not show parts of the body that lemongrass has been proven to treat.

Research Area 01

Cardiovascular outcomes

Lemongrass, lemongrass tea and citral have been investigated in relation to blood pressure and cardiovascular physiology.

Research Area 02

Nervous system & aroma

Human and preclinical research has examined lemongrass aroma, behavioural responses and anxiety-related outcomes. Aroma studies are not evidence about drinking the tea.

Research Area 03

Antimicrobial & inflammatory research

Extracts, essential oils and isolated compounds have been investigated extensively in laboratory settings. Such findings should not automatically be transferred to normal tea consumption.

07 • What Modern Research Says

Interesting research. Limited conclusions.

Lemongrass has an extensive traditional record and substantial experimental research. Human clinical evidence, however, remains comparatively limited.

Human Research Exists

Blood pressure

Small human studies have investigated lemongrass tea and blood-pressure outcomes. Reviews nevertheless conclude that the potential has not yet been thoroughly established.

Mixed / Limited

Relaxation & anxiety

Research involving lemongrass aroma has produced some interesting findings, while older oral-infusion research did not demonstrate hypnotic or anxiolytic effects. Preparation matters.

Mostly Laboratory

Antimicrobial activity

Essential oil and extracts have demonstrated activity in experimental settings. This does not establish equivalent antimicrobial effects from an ordinary cup of lemongrass tea.

08 • Three Ways To Read The Evidence

Tradition. Science. The unknown.

One of the Rootz Library's most important principles is knowing which is which.

Rooted In Tradition

What communities have done.

Lemongrass has been brewed as an aromatic household tea and incorporated into Caribbean, African and Asian herbal traditions.

Studied By Science

What researchers have investigated.

Researchers have explored cardiovascular, antimicrobial, inflammatory and nervous-system outcomes using different lemongrass preparations.

Not Yet Proven

What should not be overstated.

The evidence does not justify presenting lemongrass tea as a treatment for fever, hypertension, infection, anxiety or other medical conditions.

10 • How To Make Lemongrass Tea

A simple aromatic infusion.

Traditional preparation varies between households, communities and regions. The method below is a simple culinary infusion, not a medical dosage.

Simple lemongrass tea

Wash fresh lemongrass and cut it into pieces, or use clean dried leaves.

Lightly bruise or crush fresh stalks to release their aroma, then place the lemongrass in a heat-safe cup or pot.

Cover with freshly boiled water and allow the infusion to develop until the aroma and flavour reach the strength you prefer.

Step 01 Wash and cut fresh lemongrass, or prepare dried leaves.
Step 02 Lightly bruise fresh stalks to release more aroma.
Step 03 Cover with freshly boiled water.
Step 04 Allow the citrus aroma and flavour to develop before drinking.

11 • Lemongrass Safety

Tea, extract and essential oil are not the same thing.

Preparation and concentration matter.

Culinary lemongrass, an ordinary water infusion, concentrated extracts and essential oil represent very different levels of exposure.

A laboratory result involving concentrated essential oil should not be interpreted as a reason to swallow lemongrass essential oil.

Herbal products may interact with medicines, and individual sensitivity varies.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, speak with an appropriate healthcare professional before using concentrated herbal preparations.

The Rootz Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace medical advice.

12 • Questions People Ask About Lemongrass

The questions behind the searches.

Clear answers to common questions about lemongrass, fever grass and lemongrass tea.

What is lemongrass?
Lemongrass is Cymbopogon citratus, an aromatic perennial grass in the Poaceae family. Its leaves and lower stalks are widely used in cooking and herbal infusions.
Is lemongrass the same as fever grass?
In many Caribbean communities, Cymbopogon citratus is commonly known as fever grass. Common names can vary between islands and communities, so botanical identification remains important.
What is lemongrass tea good for?
Lemongrass has a long history as an aromatic household infusion. Research has examined cardiovascular, antimicrobial, inflammatory and nervous-system outcomes. However, much of that research is preclinical or uses extracts, oils or concentrations that differ from ordinary tea.
What are lemongrass tea benefits?
The phrase “lemongrass tea benefits” is often used broadly online. A more accurate interpretation is that lemongrass tea is an aromatic, caffeine-free herbal infusion, while specific health effects require evidence from appropriate human studies.
How do you prepare lemongrass?
Fresh lemongrass can be washed, cut and lightly bruised before being used in food or herbal infusions. Dried lemongrass can also be steeped directly in hot water.
How do you make lemongrass tea?
Cut or lightly bruise fresh lemongrass, or use dried leaves. Cover with freshly boiled water and allow it to infuse until the desired flavour and aroma develop.
Where does lemongrass come from?
Cymbopogon citratus is native to South India and Sri Lanka. It has since been introduced widely across tropical regions, including numerous African and Caribbean territories.
Is lemongrass tea the same as lemongrass essential oil?
No. Herbal tea is a water-based infusion. Essential oil is a highly concentrated aromatic preparation. Research and safety information about one should not automatically be applied to the other.
Is there a best time to drink lemongrass tea?
There is currently no well-established universal time of day at which ordinary lemongrass tea provides greater general health benefit. Timing is therefore mainly a matter of preference, tolerance and personal ritual.
