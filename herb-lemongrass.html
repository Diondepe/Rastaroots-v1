Lemongrass

Cymbopogon citratus

Citrusy • Fresh • Grassy

Bright, aromatic and unmistakably fresh. Lemongrass is a tropical perennial grass prized for its citrus-like aroma and used in food, tea and plant traditions across South Asia, Africa, the Caribbean and many other tropical regions.

Lemongrass in tradition and modern research

Lemongrass is native to South India and Sri Lanka but is now cultivated widely across tropical regions. Its leaves are commonly used to create aromatic infusions and flavour food. Traditional uses are extensive, particularly across tropical communities, while modern human clinical research remains much more limited than the traditional record.

01 • Know The Grass

What exactly is lemongrass?

Lemongrass is a perennial aromatic grass in the Poaceae family. It grows in dense clumps of long, narrow leaves rising from pale green basal stalks.

The plant's distinctive aroma comes from volatile compounds, particularly citral, which contribute its bright lemon-like fragrance.

Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew recognises Cymbopogon citratus as an accepted species native to South India and Sri Lanka.

Lemongrass carries citrus without being citrus — a grass transformed by aroma.

03 • African & Caribbean Heritage

From tropical grass to household tea.

Cymbopogon citratus is native to South India and Sri Lanka, but it has been introduced widely across the tropical world.

It became established in regions of Africa, Central and South America and throughout the Caribbean, where warm climates support its vigorous growth.

In Caribbean herbal traditions, lemongrass — often called fever grass — became a familiar household plant brewed as bush tea. Ethnobotanical research from Trinidad records Cymbopogon citratus among frequently cited medicinal plants.

South Asian Roots

Kew records South India and Sri Lanka as the plant's native range.

Tropical Africa

Lemongrass became cultivated across many tropical African countries, entering food and household plant traditions.

Caribbean

The plant became established across numerous Caribbean islands and is strongly associated with household bush teas.

Jamaica

Lemongrass is established in Jamaica and forms part of wider Caribbean fever-grass and tea traditions.

05 • Lemongrass Research Areas

Where researchers have looked.

Lemongrass research spans laboratory, animal and human studies. These areas show what has been investigated, not conditions that lemongrass has been proven to treat.

Research Area 01

Cardiovascular research

Lemongrass and its constituents have been investigated in relation to blood pressure and cardiovascular markers. Human evidence remains limited.

Research Area 02

Nervous system & behaviour

Laboratory and preclinical studies have examined effects related to the nervous system, relaxation and behaviour. These findings should not be interpreted as clinical proof.

Research Area 03

Antimicrobial & inflammatory research

Extracts and lemongrass essential oil have been widely investigated in laboratory research. Laboratory activity does not automatically predict an effect from drinking lemongrass tea.

06 • What Modern Research Says

Interesting research. Limited conclusions.

Lemongrass has a substantial traditional-use record and considerable laboratory research. Human clinical evidence, however, remains comparatively limited.

Human Research Limited

Blood pressure

Cardiovascular effects of lemongrass and citral have been investigated, but reviews note that this area has not yet been thoroughly studied in humans.

Mostly Preclinical

Inflammation & oxidative stress

Laboratory and animal research has examined several biological mechanisms. That is not equivalent to demonstrating a clinical benefit from ordinary lemongrass tea.

Laboratory Evidence

Antimicrobial activity

Lemongrass essential oil and extracts show activity against microorganisms in experimental settings. Concentrated oils are very different from a brewed leaf infusion.

Human research limited

Some human investigation exists, but the evidence base is not yet extensive.

Mostly preclinical

Evidence comes mainly from laboratory or animal research.

Preparation matters

Essential oil, extracts and ordinary tea cannot be treated as interchangeable.

09 • The Rootz Ritual

Freshness belongs in the cup.

A Simple Lemongrass Ritual

Cut or gently bruise fresh lemongrass stalks, or use dried leaves. Cover with freshly boiled water and allow the infusion to release its citrus aroma.

Before drinking, notice how the fragrance reaches you before the flavour. Sip slowly and let the clean finish develop across the cup.

13 • Lemongrass Safety

Tea, extract and essential oil are not the same thing.

Preparation matters.

Culinary lemongrass, ordinary herbal infusions, concentrated extracts and essential oil are different preparations and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Much of the laboratory research on lemongrass uses extracts or essential oil at concentrations very different from those found in a normal cup of tea.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, speak to an appropriate healthcare professional before using concentrated herbal preparations or essential oils.

Essential oils should not be casually swallowed simply because the source plant is used as food or tea.

The Rootz Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace medical advice.
