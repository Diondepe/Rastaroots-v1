Lemongrass At A Glance

What is lemongrass?

Lemongrass, sometimes written lemon grass, is Cymbopogon citratus, an aromatic perennial grass in the Poaceae family. Its long leaves and lower stalks release a distinctive citrus-like aroma and are used in food, drinks and herbal tea.

Lemongrass benefits, lemongrass tea and fever grass

Lemongrass is now cultivated across many tropical regions and is used in food, aromatic preparations and herbal infusions around the world.

In Caribbean communities, Cymbopogon citratus is widely associated with the traditional name fever grass and with household bush-tea culture.

01 • Know The Grass

What exactly is lemongrass?

Lemongrass is a clump-forming, aromatic perennial grass recognised botanically as Cymbopogon citratus.

Its long leaves and pale lower stalks contain volatile aromatic compounds. Citral is strongly associated with the familiar lemon-like fragrance that gives the plant its common English name.

Lemongrass can be used fresh or dried. Different preparations include whole leaves, sliced stalks, herbal tea, culinary preparations, extracts and essential oil. These forms are not interchangeable.

Citrus without citrus — a tropical grass remembered by its aroma.

02 • Lemongrass Benefits

What are the benefits of lemongrass?

Lemongrass has an extensive history of traditional and culinary use. Modern researchers have investigated several biological and clinical outcomes, but evidence strength varies considerably.

Tea is not essential oil.

Studies involving concentrated extracts, essential oil, isolated citral, laboratory cells or animals cannot automatically be used to claim the same effect from drinking an ordinary cup of lemongrass tea.

Human Research Exists

Cardiovascular outcomes

Human and experimental research has examined lemongrass in relation to blood pressure and cardiovascular measures. Current evidence is not strong enough to present lemongrass tea as a treatment for hypertension.

Mostly Preclinical

Antimicrobial & inflammatory research

Lemongrass extracts, essential oils and isolated compounds have been investigated for antimicrobial and inflammatory activity. Much of this work remains laboratory or animal research.

Traditional Use

Herbal tea & relaxation ritual

Lemongrass is widely enjoyed as a fragrant caffeine-free infusion. Its place in relaxation and evening traditions is culturally significant, but should not be converted into unsupported claims about treating anxiety or insomnia.

04 • Africa, Caribbean & Fever Grass

A travelled grass. A Caribbean tradition.

Lemongrass travelled far beyond its Asian botanical origins through cultivation, trade and human movement.

The plant became established throughout numerous tropical regions, including parts of Africa, Central and South America and the Caribbean.

Across Caribbean communities, Cymbopogon citratus became familiar as fever grass and as a household bush-tea ingredient.

That story matters: botanical origin and cultural belonging are not the same thing. A plant can travel and become deeply woven into the daily practices of another region.

Asian Origins

Lemongrass belongs to a broader tropical Asian botanical story.

Tropical Africa

Cultivation introduced lemongrass into numerous African food and plant traditions.

Caribbean

Tropical growing conditions helped lemongrass become established throughout the region.

Fever Grass

The name preserves part of the plant's Caribbean cultural history, without constituting proof of a medical effect.

06 • Lemongrass Research Areas

Where researchers have looked.

These markers represent areas researchers have investigated. They do not represent body systems that lemongrass has been proven to treat.

Research Area 01

Cardiovascular outcomes

Researchers have examined lemongrass, lemongrass tea and citral in relation to blood pressure and cardiovascular physiology.

Research Area 02

Nervous system & aroma

Human and preclinical research has investigated lemongrass aroma, behavioural responses and anxiety-related outcomes. Aroma studies are not evidence about drinking lemongrass tea.

Research Area 03

Antimicrobial & inflammatory research

Extracts, essential oils and isolated compounds have been investigated extensively in laboratory settings. These findings should not automatically be transferred to normal tea consumption.

07 • What Modern Research Says

Interesting research. Limited conclusions.

Lemongrass has extensive traditional use and substantial experimental research. Human clinical evidence remains much more limited for many popular lemongrass benefit claims.

Human Research Exists

Blood pressure

Small human studies have investigated lemongrass tea and cardiovascular measures. Current evidence does not justify replacing established blood-pressure treatment with lemongrass.

Mixed / Limited

Relaxation & anxiety

Research involving lemongrass aroma has produced interesting findings, while evidence from oral preparations remains limited and preparation-dependent.

Mostly Laboratory

Antimicrobial activity

Essential oil and extracts have demonstrated activity in experimental settings. This does not establish equivalent antimicrobial effects from drinking an ordinary cup of lemongrass tea.

09 • How To Prepare Lemongrass

How do you prepare and use lemongrass?

Preparation depends on whether lemongrass is being used for tea, cooking or another culinary purpose. Fresh and dried material behave differently.

01 • Wash

Clean Lemongrass

Wash fresh stalks and leaves carefully before preparation. Remove dry or damaged outer material where needed.

02 • Cut

Cut or Slice

For tea, fresh lemongrass can be cut into manageable pieces. For cooking, recipes may call for sliced, chopped or whole bruised stalks.

03 • Bruise

Release The Aroma

Lightly crushing or bruising the tougher stalk helps release the plant's citrus-like aromatic compounds.

04 • Use

Tea Or Food

Lemongrass can be used in herbal infusions, soups, curries, sauces, marinades and other culinary preparations.

Dried lemongrass: Dried leaves can generally be used directly for herbal tea without bruising. Their flavour and aroma differ from fresh stalks.

10 • Lemongrass Tea

How to make lemongrass tea.

Lemongrass tea can be prepared from fresh stalks and leaves or from dried lemongrass. Traditional methods vary between households, communities and regions.

Simple lemongrass tea

Wash and cut fresh lemongrass, or prepare clean dried leaves.

If using fresh stalks, lightly bruise or crush them to release more of their citrus aroma.

Place the lemongrass in a heat-safe cup or pot and cover with freshly boiled water.

Allow the infusion to develop until the aroma and flavour reach the strength you prefer.

Step 02 • Bruise Lightly bruise fresh stalks to release more aroma.
Step 03 • Infuse Cover the lemongrass with freshly boiled water.
Step 04 • Taste Allow the citrus aroma and flavour to develop to your preferred strength.

11 • Lemongrass Safety & Side Effects

Tea, extract and essential oil are not the same.

Preparation and concentration matter.

Culinary lemongrass, an ordinary water infusion, concentrated extracts and lemongrass essential oil represent very different preparations and levels of exposure.

Laboratory findings involving concentrated essential oil should not be interpreted as evidence that drinking ordinary lemongrass tea produces the same effect.

Essential oils should not be treated as interchangeable with herbal tea. Concentrated products can carry different safety considerations.

Herbal products may interact with medicines, and individual sensitivity varies.

If you are pregnant, trying to become pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, speak with an appropriate healthcare professional before using concentrated herbal preparations.

The Rootz Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace medical advice.

12 • Questions People Ask About Lemongrass

Lemongrass questions, answered clearly.

Practical answers about lemongrass, lemon grass, fever grass, preparation, tea and safety.

What is lemongrass?
Lemongrass is Cymbopogon citratus, an aromatic perennial grass in the Poaceae family. Its leaves and lower stalks are widely used in cooking, drinks and herbal infusions.
Is lemon grass the same as lemongrass?
Yes. “Lemongrass” is the standard spelling, while “lemon grass” is also frequently used in everyday searches and writing. This page refers specifically to Cymbopogon citratus.
What are the benefits of lemongrass?
Lemongrass has extensive traditional use and researchers have investigated cardiovascular, antimicrobial, inflammatory and nervous-system outcomes. Much of the broader evidence involves extracts, essential oils, animals or laboratory research, so specific medical benefits from ordinary lemongrass tea should not be overstated.
What are lemongrass tea benefits?
Lemongrass tea is an aromatic, naturally caffeine-free herbal infusion. Human research has investigated some outcomes, including cardiovascular measures, but evidence remains limited. Research involving extracts or essential oils should not automatically be applied to ordinary lemongrass tea.
What is lemongrass tea good for?
Traditionally, lemongrass tea has been used as an aromatic household infusion across many cultures. Modern research has investigated several possible effects, but current evidence does not establish lemongrass tea as a treatment for disease.
Is lemongrass the same as fever grass?
In many Caribbean communities, Cymbopogon citratus is commonly called fever grass. The traditional name is culturally important, but it does not itself prove that lemongrass treats fever.
How do you prepare lemongrass?
Wash fresh lemongrass, remove damaged or very dry outer material, cut it into manageable pieces and lightly bruise the stalk if you want to release more aroma. Dried lemongrass leaves can generally be used directly in herbal infusions.
How do you use lemongrass?
Lemongrass can be used for herbal tea and in soups, curries, marinades, sauces and other culinary preparations. Tough stalks may be bruised for flavour and removed before serving, depending on the recipe.
How do you cook with lemongrass?
Fresh lemongrass can be trimmed, sliced, finely chopped or bruised, depending on the dish. The tender inner section is often used when finely slicing, while tougher stalks may be bruised and simmered to release flavour before being removed.
How do you make lemongrass tea?
Cut or lightly bruise fresh lemongrass, or use dried leaves. Place it in a heat-safe cup or pot, cover with freshly boiled water and allow the citrus aroma and flavour to develop.
Can you use dried lemongrass for tea?
Yes. Clean dried lemongrass is widely used for herbal infusions. Its flavour profile differs somewhat from fresh stalks, but both forms can produce a recognisable citrus-grass aroma.
Where does lemongrass come from?
Lemongrass has tropical Asian botanical origins and has been introduced and cultivated throughout numerous tropical regions, including Africa and the Caribbean.
Is lemongrass tea the same as lemongrass essential oil?
No. Lemongrass tea is a water-based infusion. Lemongrass essential oil is a highly concentrated aromatic preparation. Their concentrations, uses and safety considerations are very different.
Can lemongrass cause side effects?
Individual tolerance varies, and concentrated products should not be treated as equivalent to culinary use or ordinary tea. Anyone who is pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication or managing a medical condition should seek appropriate professional advice before using concentrated herbal products.
Is there a best time to drink lemongrass tea?
There is no well-established universal time of day at which ordinary lemongrass tea has been shown to provide greater general health benefit. Timing is mainly a matter of personal preference, tolerance and ritual.
