Quick answer

What is tulsi tea good for?

Tulsi, or holy basil (Ocimum tenuiflorum), is a sacred plant in Hinduism, brewed as a peppery, clove-scented, caffeine-free tea. In Ayurveda it is used for stress and resilience. Some small studies have looked at stress and blood sugar, but they are small and of poor quality, and there are no high-quality trials showing it treats any condition.

Side effects & who should take care

  • It appears safe for most adults for up to about 8 weeks; longer use has not been studied.
  • Avoid it if you are pregnant or trying to get pregnant.
  • It may slow blood clotting and might lower thyroid hormone levels.
  • It is not the same as sweet basil used in cooking.

Tulsi At A Glance

What is tulsi?

Tulsi is a small, bushy herb in the mint family, related to sweet basil but with a spicier, clove-like scent.

Botanical Name Ocimum tenuiflorum (also O. sanctum)
Family Lamiaceae (mint family)
Part Used Leaves
Character Peppery, clove-like
Native Range Indian subcontinent and tropical Asia

Tulsi, the Indo-Caribbean and the evidence

Tulsi means “the incomparable one” and is sacred in Hinduism.

Indian indentured workers carried the tradition to the Caribbean, and tulsi now grows in Hindu yards across Trinidad, Guyana and Suriname.

Evidence for health benefits in people is limited and mostly from small, poor-quality studies.

01 • Know The Herb

Not your kitchen basil.

Tulsi is a bushy herb with soft, slightly toothed leaves and spikes of small purple or pink flowers.

There are green (Rama) and purple (Krishna) types. Both are used for tea.

It is a close relative of sweet basil (Ocimum basilicum), but tastes peppery and clove-like rather than sweet.

Its leaves contain aromatic compounds such as eugenol, also found in cloves.

A sacred plant at the door. A peppery leaf in the cup.

02 • Tulsi Benefits

What is tulsi tea good for?

Tulsi is called an “adaptogen” in Ayurveda. That is a traditional idea, not a proven effect.

The honest picture: a sacred, warming cup with weak evidence.

Small human studies have looked at tulsi for stress, anxiety and blood sugar, but many are small and of poor quality, and there are no high-quality studies showing it treats any condition. Animal studies suggest other effects, but these have not been confirmed in people.

04 • Indo-Caribbean Heritage

From India to Trinidad and Guyana yards.

Tulsi has been sacred in India for thousands of years and is grown at homes and temples.

From the 1830s, Indian indentured workers were brought to the Caribbean. They carried their faith, festivals and plants, including tulsi.

Today a tulsi plant stands in the yard of almost every Hindu home in Trinidad and Guyana, often beside colourful jhandi prayer flags.

10 • How To Make Tulsi Tea

How to make tulsi tea.

Fresh or dried both work. Cover the cup to keep the aroma.

Tulsi tea

Put 1 teaspoon of dried tulsi, or 8 to 10 fresh leaves, in a cup.

Pour over 250 ml of just-boiled water.

Cover and steep for 5 to 7 minutes, then strain.

Add honey, lemon or a slice of fresh ginger if you like.

Step 01 • Measure 1 tsp dried or 8–10 fresh leaves.
Step 02 • Pour 250 ml just-boiled water.
Step 03 • Steep 5–7 minutes, covered.
Step 04 • Finish Honey, lemon or ginger.

11 • Tulsi Side Effects & Safety

Gentle short-term, with cautions.

Pregnancy, blood clotting, thyroid and blood sugar.

Tulsi appears safe for most adults when taken for up to about 8 weeks. Longer use has not been studied. It can cause nausea or diarrhoea.

Pregnancy: tulsi might not be safe if you are pregnant or trying to get pregnant. Safety in breastfeeding has not been studied.

Blood clotting: it might slow clotting, so take care with blood thinners and stop before surgery.

Thyroid and blood sugar: it might lower thyroid hormone levels and blood sugar. Ask a pharmacist if you take thyroid or diabetes medicines.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, speak to a pharmacist before using tulsi regularly if you take blood thinners, thyroid or diabetes medicines.

The Rootz Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace medical advice.

12 • Questions People Ask About Tulsi

Tulsi questions, answered clearly.

Straightforward answers to the questions people search for most.

What is tulsi tea good for?
It is a traditional Ayurvedic tea for stress and resilience. Human evidence is limited and mostly from small studies.
Is tulsi the same as holy basil?
Yes. Tulsi is the Hindi name for holy basil, Ocimum tenuiflorum.
Is holy basil the same as sweet basil?
No. They are related, but holy basil is spicier and is a different species.
How do you make tulsi tea?
Steep 1 teaspoon of dried tulsi or 8 to 10 fresh leaves in 250 ml of just-boiled water for 5 to 7 minutes, covered.
Why do Hindu families grow tulsi?
Tulsi is sacred in Hinduism and is grown at homes and temples, including across Trinidad and Guyana.
Is tulsi tea safe in pregnancy?
It is best avoided if you are pregnant or trying to get pregnant.
Can I drink tulsi tea every day?
It appears safe for up to about 8 weeks. Longer use has not been studied.
Does tulsi tea contain caffeine?
No. Pure tulsi tea is caffeine-free. Blends with green tea contain caffeine.
Is tulsi an adaptogen?
“Adaptogen” is a traditional idea. There is no strong evidence it works that way in people.
Can tulsi interact with medicines?
It might affect blood clotting, blood sugar and thyroid levels. Ask a pharmacist.
