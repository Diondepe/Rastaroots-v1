Mint At A Glance

What is mint?

Mint refers to plants in the Mentha genus. This Library entry focuses on spearmint, Mentha spicata, one of the most familiar culinary and tea mints.

Botanical Name Mentha spicata
Family Lamiaceae

Mint in tradition and modern research

Spearmint has a long history as a culinary and aromatic herb and is now cultivated far beyond its native range. Its fresh leaves are widely used in food, tea and drinks. Mint research covers several different species and preparations, so evidence relating to peppermint oil, spearmint extract and ordinary mint tea should not be treated as interchangeable.

01 • Know The Leaf

What exactly is mint?

Mint is not a single plant. Mentha is a genus containing numerous species and naturally occurring hybrids. This RastaRoots entry focuses on spearmint, Mentha spicata.

Spearmint is a perennial herb in the Lamiaceae family. Its leaves are typically green, aromatic and toothed, and the plant spreads readily through underground stems.

Kew records Mentha spicata as an accepted species with a native range extending from Europe to China. Today it is cultivated and naturalised far beyond that original range.

Mint doesn't need to shout. Its aroma reaches you first.

03 • African & Caribbean Heritage

A travelled herb. A local tradition.

Spearmint did not begin in the Caribbean. Its botanical range reaches across Europe and Asia, while related mint species also occur across parts of Africa.

Through cultivation, migration and trade, mints travelled widely and became embedded in local food, tea and household traditions.

Across Caribbean communities, mint became one of many aromatic plants used in teas and drinks. What began elsewhere became part of local flavour, family knowledge and everyday life.

Europe & Asia

Kew records the native range of spearmint from Europe to China.

Africa

Mint species have long been grown and used across African regions, especially in tea, food and aromatic preparations.

Caribbean

Introduced mints became established in tropical gardens and household food and tea traditions.

Shared Knowledge

Mint illustrates how a plant can travel between regions while becoming meaningful in different local cultures.

05 • Mint Research Areas

Where researchers have looked.

Research involving mint includes different species, preparations and doses. These areas show what has been studied, not what an ordinary cup of mint tea has been proven to treat.

Research Area 01

Digestion & gastrointestinal research

Mint species have been studied in relation to gastrointestinal function. Some of the better-known human evidence concerns specific peppermint-oil products, not spearmint tea.

Research Area 02

Cognition & memory

Proprietary spearmint extracts have been investigated in small controlled human studies involving working memory and cognitive performance. These findings relate to specific extracts and doses.

Research Area 03

Hormonal outcomes

Small human studies have investigated spearmint tea in relation to androgen levels in women. This remains a limited research area and does not justify broad hormonal treatment claims.

06 • What Modern Research Says

Species matters. Preparation matters.

“Mint research” is not one evidence base. Spearmint, peppermint, essential oils, concentrated extracts and ordinary leaf tea may produce very different results.

Limited Human Evidence

Spearmint & hormones

Small studies have reported changes in some androgen measurements following spearmint tea consumption. The studies were limited, and this should not be interpreted as evidence that mint tea treats hormonal disorders.

Specific Extract Research

Memory & cognition

Controlled trials have investigated proprietary spearmint extracts rich in rosmarinic acid for cognitive performance. Results from a concentrated extract cannot automatically be transferred to an ordinary cup of mint tea.

Different Mint Species

Digestive research

Some comparatively stronger clinical research exists for enteric-coated peppermint oil in IBS. Peppermint oil is not the same preparation or species focus as this spearmint entry.

Species matters

Evidence for one mint species should not automatically be applied to every other mint.

Preparation matters

Tea, essential oil and concentrated extracts can be substantially different.

Evidence quality matters

Small studies may justify further research without establishing a treatment claim.

09 • The Rootz Ritual

A Simple Mint Ritual

Take fresh mint leaves and gently bruise them between your fingers, or use dried leaf. Cover with freshly boiled water and allow the aroma to rise as the leaves infuse.

Before drinking, breathe in. Notice the difference between the fragrance of the leaf and the flavour that follows in the cup.

13 • Mint Safety

Leaf tea and essential oil are not the same thing.

Species, dose and preparation matter.

Culinary mint leaves, ordinary herbal infusions, concentrated extracts and mint essential oils are different preparations.

Evidence involving peppermint oil or concentrated spearmint extracts should not automatically be interpreted as evidence about an ordinary cup of mint tea.

Concentrated peppermint oil can cause unwanted effects and may worsen heartburn in some people.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, speak to an appropriate healthcare professional before using concentrated herbal preparations or essential oils.

The Rootz Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace medical advice.
