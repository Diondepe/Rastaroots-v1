01 • Know The Root

What exactly is ginger?

Ginger is a perennial plant in the Zingiberaceae family. The part most commonly eaten and brewed is its underground rhizome — the knotted, aromatic structure usually sold as “ginger root.”

Fresh ginger produces a bright, pungent heat. Drying changes its aromatic profile and can produce a sharper, more concentrated spice character.

Kew records Zingiber officinale as native from the Eastern Himalaya to south-central China. Centuries of cultivation and exchange have since made ginger part of food and plant traditions across tropical regions worldwide.

A plant can begin in one part of the world and become part of another culture's everyday life.

03 • African & Caribbean Heritage

A travelled root. A local tradition.

Ginger did not originate in the Caribbean or Africa. Its botanical history begins in Asia, but centuries of cultivation, trade and cultural exchange carried it far beyond its native range.

It became cultivated across tropical Africa and the Caribbean. Kew specifically records commercial cultivation in tropical Africa and highlights Jamaica within the Caribbean.

Across Caribbean communities, ginger appears in teas, juices, cooking, seasonal drinks and household herbal traditions. Its modern role is therefore more than imported botanical history: it forms part of living regional food culture.

Africa

Ginger is cultivated across tropical Africa and has been incorporated into culinary and traditional plant practices.

Caribbean

Ginger is established within Caribbean food, drink and household plant traditions alongside other culturally important botanicals.

Jamaica

Jamaica is particularly associated with commercial ginger cultivation and strongly aromatic ginger products.

04 • The Journey Of Ginger

Plants travel. Cultures make them their own.

Ginger's historical movement was complex, involving multiple cultivation and trade networks. This map shows a broad cultural journey rather than claiming one single chronological route.

Kew records Zingiber officinale as native from the Eastern Himalaya to south-central China and widely cultivated across tropical Africa and the Caribbean, including Jamaica. 05 • Inside The Body

Where researchers have looked.

This does not show organs that ginger “treats.” It highlights body systems and outcomes that have been investigated in human or biomedical research.

Research Area 01

Digestive & nausea research

Nausea and vomiting are among the most researched clinical areas for ginger, although results depend on the cause of nausea, dose and preparation.

Research Area 02

Muscles, joints & discomfort

Ginger has been investigated in research involving pain, muscle soreness and osteoarthritis-related outcomes. Research is not equivalent to a treatment claim.

Research Area 03

Metabolic & inflammatory markers

Researchers have studied ginger in relation to glucose, lipids and inflammatory biomarkers, but evidence quality and effect sizes vary.

06 • What Modern Research Says

Evidence, not exaggeration.

Ginger has been investigated across many outcomes. Some areas have stronger evidence than others, and findings from supplements or extracts cannot automatically be transferred to a normal cup of tea.

Better Studied

Pregnancy-related nausea

Research reviews suggest ginger supplements may help reduce the severity of nausea during pregnancy. Pregnancy still warrants individual healthcare advice, particularly for supplements.

Mixed / Context Dependent

Other forms of nausea

Ginger has also been investigated for postoperative, chemotherapy-related and motion-related nausea. Findings differ across conditions and study designs.

Emerging / Variable

Metabolic & inflammatory outcomes

Reviews report research into glucose control, lipids, pain and inflammatory markers. Evidence strength varies substantially by outcome.

Better studied

Supported by multiple human studies or reviews, with comparatively stronger evidence for the specific outcome described.

Mixed / context dependent

Human research exists, but results vary by population, preparation, dose or study design.

Emerging / variable

An active research area where findings are interesting but not strong enough for firm conclusions.

Culinary Use

Ginger remains as much a culinary ingredient as a herbal one — appearing across sweet, savoury and beverage traditions.

Simple Ginger Infusion

A straightforward food-style preparation for exploring ginger's aroma and flavour at home. This is a culinary ritual, not a medical dose.

Prepare Thinly slice or lightly crush a small piece of fresh ginger.
Steep Cover with freshly boiled water and allow the flavour to develop to your preference.
Make It Yours Add lemon or honey for flavour if you wish. Sip slowly and notice the aroma and heat.

13 • Safety & Considerations

Natural does not mean consequence-free.

Ginger is food — but dose and form matter.

Ginger used in ordinary food and drink is not automatically equivalent to concentrated extracts or dietary supplements used in clinical studies.

NCCIH notes that oral ginger can cause effects such as abdominal discomfort, heartburn, diarrhoea, and mouth or throat irritation in some people.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, speak with an appropriate healthcare professional before using concentrated herbal supplements.

This Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace individual medical advice.
