01 • Know The Root

What exactly is ginger?

Ginger is a perennial plant in the Zingiberaceae family. The part most commonly eaten and brewed is its underground rhizome — the knotted, aromatic structure usually sold as “ginger root.”

Fresh ginger produces a bright, pungent heat. Drying changes its aromatic profile and can produce a sharper, more concentrated spice character.

Today it is cultivated throughout many tropical regions and has become deeply embedded in food, drink and herbal traditions far beyond its earliest Asian range.

A plant can begin in one part of the world and become part of another culture's everyday life.

03 • African & Caribbean Heritage

A travelled root. A local tradition.

Ginger did not originate in the Caribbean or Africa. Its botanical history begins in Asia, but centuries of cultivation, trade, migration and cultural exchange carried it far beyond its earliest range.

It became established across tropical Africa and the Caribbean, including Jamaica, where ginger developed an especially strong agricultural, culinary and beverage identity.

Across Caribbean communities, ginger appears in teas, juices, cooking, seasonal drinks and family herbal preparations. Its modern role is therefore not simply imported botanical history: it is part of living regional food culture.

Africa

Ginger became cultivated across tropical Africa and incorporated into local culinary and traditional plant practices.

Caribbean

Ginger is established within Caribbean food, beverage and household herbal traditions, alongside other culturally important plants.

Jamaica

Jamaica became particularly associated with ginger cultivation and strong, aromatic ginger products.

05 • Inside The Body

Where researchers have looked.

This does not show organs that ginger “treats.” It highlights body systems and outcomes that have been investigated in human or biomedical research.

Research Area 01

Digestive & nausea research

Nausea and vomiting are among the most extensively researched clinical uses of ginger, although results depend on the cause of nausea, dose and preparation.

Research Area 02

Muscles, joints & discomfort

Ginger has been investigated in research involving pain, muscle soreness and osteoarthritis-related outcomes. Evidence is not equivalent to a treatment claim.

Research Area 03

Metabolic & inflammatory markers

Researchers have studied ginger in relation to glucose, lipids and inflammatory biomarkers, but evidence quality varies considerably.

06 • What Modern Research Says

Evidence, not exaggeration.

Ginger has been investigated across many outcomes. Some areas have more convincing evidence than others, and results cannot automatically be transferred between food, tea, extracts and supplements.

Better Studied

Pregnancy-related nausea

Clinical research suggests ginger supplements may reduce the severity of nausea during pregnancy. Pregnancy still warrants individual medical advice, particularly for supplement use.

Mixed / Context Dependent

Other forms of nausea

Ginger has also been studied for postoperative, chemotherapy-related and motion-related nausea, but findings are not uniformly positive.

Emerging / Variable

Metabolic & inflammatory outcomes

Reviews report research into blood pressure, lipids, glucose, pain and inflammatory markers, but evidence strength ranges from low to moderate and varies by outcome.

07 • Two Ways Of Knowing

Tradition and evidence are not the same thing.

Traditional Knowledge

What communities have done.

Traditional knowledge records how people, families and cultures have prepared and understood plants over generations.

  • Household ginger teas
  • Warming preparations
  • Digestive traditions
  • Food and beverage uses
  • Family herbal knowledge
Modern Evidence

What controlled research asks.

Clinical research evaluates specific preparations, doses, populations and outcomes. Results from supplements or extracts do not automatically describe what a normal cup of herbal tea will do.

  • Study design matters
  • Dose matters
  • Preparation matters
  • Population matters
  • Evidence quality matters

13 • Safety & Considerations

Natural does not mean consequence-free.

Ginger is food — but dose and form matter.

Ginger used in normal food and drink is not automatically equivalent to taking concentrated extracts or supplements.

Reported side effects of oral ginger can include abdominal discomfort, heartburn, diarrhoea, and mouth or throat irritation.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, preparing for surgery or managing a medical condition, speak to an appropriate healthcare professional before using concentrated herbal supplements.

This Library is educational and does not diagnose, treat or replace individual medical advice.
